A corporate structure

The Equitable Corporation

A company structured so the people who create the value keep it — and the promises that protect them can only get stronger, never weaker. Not a label. A way a company is actually built.

What it is

Most of today’s tech is built on extraction: your data, attention, and effort are used to create value that flows away from you. An Equitable Corporation inverts that.

An Equitable Corporation is a company whose structure is built so the value created on it is not extracted, and whose core commitments are enforced so they can only be strengthened, never weakened. The people who put in the work keep what they created — and the company cannot be quietly sold off or rewritten to stop protecting them.

The core commitments

Every Equitable Corporation makes the same commitments, in the same locked form.

  • Your data stays yours. It is never sold, mined, or read beyond what’s needed to give you the service you asked for.
  • It can’t be bought up. Selling the company, the core IP, or the brand to a private equity firm, a venture capital fund, or a buyer who would take control of the board requires a supermajority and gives users a right of first refusal.
  • The rules can only get stronger. Weakening the data, privacy, or audit terms needs the same supermajority as a sale. A quiet policy change doesn’t count.
  • You can check it. Your IT security team can audit the deployed code, and the finances and cap table are public.
  • If it’s ever broken, you can leave. A sale to one of those buyers, or a weakening of your terms, breaches the agreement with every user — you can end it and get your data back.

How it’s enforced

Two layers. The charter stops a quiet sale; your exit right makes a sale that does happen costly and public.

  • The charter. A supermajority (not a normal majority) and a right of first refusal on a sale or change of control, a sale of the core IP, or a sale of the brand. You can’t amend these provisions alone.
  • Your right to exit. If any of the protected events happens, the agreement with every user is breached. Each user can cancel, get their data back, and keep their signed work valid. We don’t claim a sale is impossible — we make it costly and public.
  • You keep the software. If the price is raised (it’s fixed, inflation-pegged), the data policy is weakened, the company is sold to PE/VC, or it ceases operations — your license becomes perpetual, irrevocable, and free, and the other trusted signing authorities carry on. No renegotiation, no consent.

How it’s held

The structure is built to hold, not to be sold out from under the people it’s for.

The founder owns 100% of the equity at inception and takes no outside capital. The founder is paid a board-approved salary capped at a published benchmark, and receives distributions of profits per a locked ratio. The 1% certification fee comes from other companies adopting the Standard — it is not the founder’s pay. The founder can be replaced by an equitable vote of the community, so the structure doesn’t depend on one person staying forever.

Why a corporation, not a non-profit. The structure needs real upkeep, legal standing, and earnings to stay afloat against other companies. A non-profit would cap the founder’s earning potential and can’t compete the way a for-profit can. The Equitable Corporation lets a company sustain itself and compete — while its core commitments are enforced in two layers that only ever get stronger.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an Equitable Corporation?

A corporate structure in which the value created by users is not extracted: user data stays owned by the user, and the charter requires a supermajority and a right of first refusal for any sale of control, the core IP, or the brand to a private equity firm, a venture capital fund, or any acquirer that would control the board — so a capture is priced and public, not possible to do quietly.

How is it different from a normal company?

In a normal company, user data and attention are assets that can be sold, and the company can be sold to an acquirer. In an Equitable Corporation, user data stays owned by the user, and the charter requires a supermajority and a right of first refusal for any sale or change of control to a private equity firm, a venture capital fund, or any acquirer that would control the board — so a capture is priced and public. Permitted acquirers are limited to a mission-aligned for-profit research institution that adopts the principles in writing, a founder- and staff-directed buyout, or a dissolution into a non-profit or research-purpose trust.

Is it public or private?

Both — and that’s the point. The commitments themselves are public and verifiable: every line of deployed code is auditable by your IT security team, and governance, financials, and data practices are open to audit. What stays private is the user’s own work and data — those are never the company’s to disclose or use. And the protection is two layers: the charter makes capture costly and public, and a capture or an adverse change breaches the agreement with every signee (terminating the service and returning the data).

Who owns the value it creates?

The people who created it. User data and work stay owned by the user, and the company’s structure is built so that value isn’t redirected to outside extractors. The founder holds the equity and is paid a capped salary plus a locked ratio of profits — but cannot take the company’s defining commitments with them, because those are locked in the charter and can only be strengthened.

What stops the founder from changing the rules?

The charter. The core terms can only be strengthened, never weakened — a material adverse change to them (weakening data ownership, enabling sale/mining of the signee’s data or records, or reducing auditability) requires the stated supermajority and triggers the per-signee remedy. A company can add features and grow, but a material adverse change to those principles requires the stated supermajority.

Is the founder above the company?

No. The founder can be replaced by an equitable vote of the community, so the structure doesn’t depend on one person staying forever. And because the commitments are in the charter and the user agreements, they outlive any single person, board, or management team.
